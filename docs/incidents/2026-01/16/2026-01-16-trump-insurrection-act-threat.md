## Summary
President Trump threatened to invoke the Insurrection Act to deploy military forces to Minneapolis, warning he would use the law if "corrupt politicians" don't "obey the law and stop the professional agitators and insurrectionists." The Pentagon subsequently placed 1,500 active-duty troops from the 11th Airborne Division in Alaska on standby for possible deployment. ## Background

### The Insurrection Act
The Insurrection Act of 1807 is a rarely used statute that grants a president authority to deploy federal troops for domestic law enforcement missions under limited circumstances. The law has been invoked most frequently during the Civil Rights era, particularly to enforce court-ordered desegregation and quell large-scale unrest. It has not been invoked in the 21st century.

### The Threat
On Thursday, January 16, 2026, President Trump threatened to invoke the Insurrection Act to deploy military forces to Minnesota. He warned he would use the law if "corrupt politicians" don't "obey the law and stop the professional agitators and insurrectionists."

### Military Preparations
In response to Trump's threat, the Pentagon ordered approximately 1,500 active-duty soldiers to prepare for possible deployment to Minnesota:

- **11th Airborne Division**: Two battalions from Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson in Alaska were placed on prepare-to-deploy orders. The division specializes in cold weather operations and plays a significant role in the U.S. military's posture in the Pacific.
- **Additional units**: Some 200 Texas National Guard troops and military police units from North Carolina were also directed to make preparations.

Chief Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell stated: "The Department of War is always prepared to execute the orders of the Commander-in-Chief if called upon."

### The Walk Back
By Friday, January 17, Trump appeared to walk back the threat. He told reporters that he didn't think he needed to use the Insurrection Act "right now" and that there wasn't a reason to use it "anymore."

### The Stand Down
Over the weekend of January 31 - February 1, 2026, Northern Command quietly ordered the active-duty troops to stand down. According to the New York Times, "the shooting death by immigration officers of a second U.S. citizen, Alex Pretti, on Jan. 24 galvanized public sentiment against the federal government's tactics and forced the administration to retreat."

### Local Response
**Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey** on NBC's Meet the Press (Jan 19):
> "To those that are paying attention, you've got to understand how wild this is right now. In Minneapolis, crime is dramatically down. We don't need more federal agents to keep people safe. We are safe."

Frey said deploying troops would be "not fair, it's not just, and it's completely unconstitutional."

> "You know what's causing more chaos? Having these thousands of ICE agents and Border Control and apparently military, even, potentially on our streets."

### Legal Experts
**Joseph Nunn**, attorney at the Brennan Center's Liberty and National Security Program:
> "It would be a flagrant abuse of the Insurrection Act unlike anything that's ever happened before in the history of the country."

Nunn described the Insurrection Act as a "break-glass-in-case-of-emergency type of tool" meant to be used when civilian authorities are overwhelmed by a crisis, not simply to quell protests.

## Context

### Why the 11th Airborne Division
The 11th Airborne Division is the Pentagon's primary ground combat force tailored for warfare in extreme cold. However, the unit is not built with civilian law enforcement in mind, and such a deployment would have been seen as a major escalation of the federal government's role.

The division plays a significant role in the U.S. military's posture in the Pacific, regularly training alongside allied forces as part of efforts to deter China. Deploying these troops domestically would have diverted resources from strategic military readiness.

### Public Opinion
Large shares of Americans expressed discomfort over the tactics used by immigration enforcement during this period:
- **61%** said ICE was being "too tough" when it stopped or detained people (CBS News-YouGov poll, Jan 14-16)
- **57%** disapproved of the way ICE enforces immigration laws (Quinnipiac poll)
- **82%** of respondents had seen the video of Renee Good's final moments (Quinnipiac poll)
