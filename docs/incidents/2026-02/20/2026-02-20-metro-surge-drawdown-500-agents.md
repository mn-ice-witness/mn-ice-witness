# Fewer Than 500 ICE Agents Remain; Homan Promises 150 Within a Week; Observers Report Suburban Shift

## Summary
U.S. Reps. Ilhan Omar and Angie Craig toured the Whipple Federal Building on February 20 and confirmed that fewer than 500 ICE agents remain in Minnesota, down from approximately 3,000 at the peak of Operation Metro Surge. The facility's holding cells were empty. ICE Field Office Director Sam Olson told the lawmakers that arrests had dropped to about 20 per day, deportation flights averaged two per week, and detainees were being held at four county jails. Border czar Tom Homan, who had returned to Washington, told CNN his goal was a "regular footprint" of 150 agents and that the force "should be back to regular footprint within a week." However, community observers and elected officials reported that ICE had not left the suburbs — agents had shifted to stealthier tactics including unmarked vehicles, plain clothes, smaller teams, and surveillance at bus stops, parks, and hotels across Eden Prairie, Columbia Heights, Fridley, and other communities.

## Whipple Building Visit

Omar and Craig had previously been denied access to the facility in January after failing to provide the seven-day advance notice required by a policy from Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem. This time they scheduled eight days ahead. When they arrived at noon, all detainees had been removed by 11:30 a.m.

"It seems a little too convenient knowing that our scheduled visit was going to be at 12 p.m. and that the last detainees would be transported out of the facility at 11:30," Omar said.

Craig called the seven-day notice policy unlawful and said it undermines congressional oversight. "Once again, Donald Trump's Department of Homeland Security is violating the law."

Rep. Kelly Morrison had toured the Whipple Building three times, including two surprise visits. A judge blocked DHS's seven-day notice requirement for Morrison and 12 other members of Congress earlier in the month.

## Suburban Shift

Despite the numerical drawdown, reporting by Sahan Journal and accounts from elected officials described a shift in tactics rather than a withdrawal:

**Columbia Heights / Fridley** — Observer Brenna Zeimet said ICE presence remained heavy in the days after Homan's announcement. "We still had convoys, we still had abductions those first couple days." Fridley resident Avonna Starck said agents had become "much stealthier" — wearing jeans and baseball hats instead of tactical gear, operating in smaller groups, and using older unmarked vehicles. Starck's environmental nonprofit received reports of agents posing as door-to-door canvassers.

**Eden Prairie** — An observer identified as Mary said agents were present every single day since the announcement, staging outside apartments, businesses, and city parks. "They will sometimes have multiple agents in multiple cars parked at different angles facing the bus stops," she said. Her group identified around 20 ICE vehicles routinely parked at suburban hotel lots.

**Elected officials** — State Rep. Alex Falconer (DFL-Minnetonka/Eden Prairie) said ICE followed him partway to the Capitol. "There was no license plate on the front, and occasionally he would flash his lights." State Rep. Lucy Rehm (DFL-Chanhassen) reported increased drone and helicopter activity. State Sen. Erin Maye Quade (DFL-Apple Valley) said agents followed observers to their homes and boxed them in.

Hotels remained occupied. Christa Sarrack, president of Unite Here Local 17, said at least three union hotels in Minneapolis were still housing agents.

"As far as Homan's announcement of a drawdown, there's no difference," Falconer said. "In fact, it's become a little worse."

## Context

CNN reported on February 20 that the Trump administration planned to replicate the Minneapolis enforcement model in other cities, framing Operation Metro Surge as a template rather than an anomaly. In a CNN News Central interview the same day, Homan said: "My plan is to get back to the regular footprint, which is 150, but with a couple of caveats" — noting a small security force would remain and HSI fraud investigators would stay "until the investigations are over." He added: "We should be back to regular footprint within a week." The drawdown reflected a declared mission accomplished, not a retreat — Homan told CNN the operation succeeded in making Minnesota "less of a sanctuary state" and said: "We're not surrendering our mission at all. We're just doing it smarter."

The numerical reduction from ~3,000 to ~500 agents represents the most concrete verification of the drawdown since Homan's February 12 announcement. But as Sahan Journal reported, the remaining force had adopted tactics designed to avoid the public confrontations that defined the operation's first weeks: fewer agents per team, plain clothes, unmarked vehicles, and a geographic shift from dense urban neighborhoods to suburbs where observer networks were thinner.

"The media's moving on, people are moving on like the story is closing," observer Zeimet said. "But people are still unable to leave their homes."
