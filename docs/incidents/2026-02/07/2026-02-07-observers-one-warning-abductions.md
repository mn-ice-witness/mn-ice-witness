# Three Legal Observers Arrested in 30 Minutes as ICE Implements "One Warning" Policy

## Updates
- **Feb 12** - Minnesota Reformer [investigation](https://minnesotareformer.com/2026/02/12/why-is-ice-seizing-peoples-phones-and-documents/) reveals ICE systematically seizing phones, IDs, cash, and keys from detained observers and immigrants. Attorneys report no warrants issued for seized phones. Federal government expressed interest in Signal group chats used to coordinate observer networks. 76-year-old Marine veteran Gust Johnson's phone was held at Whipple; location tracking showed someone charged it days later. Attorney Kevin Riach: "My suspicion is there are Fourth Amendment violations going on." Jennifer Scarborough called document seizures from immigrants "another form of cruelty" — replacing a work permit costs $500+ and can take a year. DHS did not respond to questions.

## Summary
On February 7, at least four legal observers — known locally as "commuters" — were arrested by federal agents in south Minneapolis over the course of an afternoon, with three taken within a 30-minute span. One female observer was on an encrypted group call with fellow commuters, pleading for help after being boxed in by agents while following a vehicle that had loaded her friend. Her pleas stopped mid-call; by the time support arrived, she was gone and her SUV sat empty in the street with the engine running. Video shot the same day by observer Kaegan Recher captured a deportation officer stating that agents have been ordered to give commuters a single warning before taking them into custody: "You just got one warning, that's it. What we're told, that's all you need." The community group Defrost MN, which uses crowdsourced data to track federal immigration operations, warned of an "uptick in abductions" following Tom Homan's takeover of Operation Metro Surge. Despite Homan's announced drawdown of 700 officers, approximately 2,000 federal personnel remain on the ground — 13 times the agencies' normal footprint and three times the size of the Minneapolis Police Department. During a press conference the same week, Homan disclosed that 158 people had been arrested for interfering with federal law enforcement in the past month, with 85 cases accepted for prosecution.

Federal authorities arrested 158 people in Minnesota for impeding or assaulting federal officers during Operation Metro Surge's first month, but when independent prosecutors and judges reviewed the evidence, the cases routinely collapsed. Of 85 cases accepted for prosecution, grand juries returned "no bills," judges dismissed charges, and felonies were downgraded to misdemeanors. A Fox 9 investigation of 92 federal cases in Chicago found zero convictions, with 20 cases declined by prosecutors and 13 dismissed after filing. University of St. Thomas law professor Rachel Moran explained: "When somebody other than the agents has to look at evidence and decide whether an assault happened, in the vast majority of situations, they're deciding, no, there's no evidence of an assault."

## What Happened

### The "One Warning" Policy
Video recorded by Kaegan Recher on February 7 shows a deportation officer explaining a new directive regarding engagement with observers: agents have been ordered to give commuters a single verbal warning before taking them into custody.

> "You just got one warning, that's it. What we're told, that's all you need."

### The Abductions
Shortly after Recher's encounter, observers on an encrypted group call received a distress call from a fellow commuter. The woman had been following federal agents who had just loaded her friend into an unmarked vehicle. She was then boxed in by federal vehicles herself.

"Please help," the woman said repeatedly, her voice rising to a scream. Then her pleas stopped.

When other commuters arrived at the scene, the observer was gone. Her SUV remained in the street, engine running.

This was at least the fourth observer taken that day, and the third in less than 30 minutes.

### Community Warning
The community group Defrost MN, which uses crowdsourced data to track federal immigration operations, circulated a warning Friday of an "uptick in abductions" — referring to arrests of both immigrant community members and observers — following Homan's takeover and an increase in the number of government personnel and vehicles involved in operations.

"National attention on Minnesota has waned with the departure of Bovino and rhetoric by Homan that things are de-escalating," the group noted, "but recent data and reports from commuters in the field did not support those conclusions."

## The Numbers

Federal authorities announced 158 arrests for impeding or assaulting federal officers in Minnesota during Operation Metro Surge's first month. Of those, 85 were accepted for prosecution. But the outcomes tell a different story.

A Fox 9 investigation reviewed 92 federal cases from ICE operations in Chicago — chosen because Minnesota cases were still too early in the process — and found:

- **54 cases:** No charges filed
- **20 cases:** Prosecutors declined to file charges
- **13 cases:** Charged, then dismissed
- **5 cases:** Charges still pending
- **3 cases:** Grand juries issued "no bills" (declined to indict)
- **0 convictions**

Federal Judge Gabriel Fuentes wrote that the U.S. Attorney's office had "swung and missed — multiple times," calling it "unusual and possibly unprecedented" for prosecutors "to charge so hastily that it either could not obtain the indictment in the grand jury or was forced to dismiss the case upon the conclusion that the case is not provable."

Nationally, at least 655 people have been charged under Title 18, Section 111 (assaulting, resisting, or impeding federal officers) since summer 2025 — more than double the prosecution rate for the same period in 2024-2025.

## The Legal Line

### What Is Protected
The First Amendment protects the right to observe, film, and follow law enforcement officers performing their duties in public. A federal court order in [Tincher v. Noem](/entry/2025-12-17-tincher-v-noem-aclu-lawsuit) specifically barred agents from "arresting, retaliating against, or pepper-spraying protesters engaged in peaceful and unobstructive protest activity." The Eighth Circuit stayed the order on December 27 but the evidentiary record remains.

Seth Stoughton, a policing professor at the University of South Carolina School of Law, told Reuters: "Without any physical contact, just following an agent in a car, it's not clear to me that that's resistance or impeding."

ACLU of Minnesota policy counsel John Boehler told The Marshall Project: "Following or observing or reporting on federal agencies... is protected First Amendment activity."

Deborah Fleischaker, a former ICE official in the Biden administration, stated: "Observing ICE activities is not a crime and should not be treated as such."

### What DHS Claims
DHS charges observers under 18 USC 111, which penalizes anyone who "forcibly assaults, resists, opposes, impedes, intimidates, or interferes with" a federal officer. Penalties range up to 20 years for felonies; the enhanced penalty beyond 8 years requires a "deadly or dangerous weapon" or bodily injury.

DHS spokesperson Tricia McLaughlin stated that those arrested had "stalked law enforcement and attempted to obstruct law enforcement from performing their sworn duties" and that agents "used the minimum amount of force necessary to protect themselves, the public, and federal property."

Border czar Tom Homan: "If you violate the law, you will be federally prosecuted."

### The Gap Between Arrest and Prosecution
The pattern emerging from case outcomes suggests a systematic disconnect between agents' characterizations of encounters and what prosecutors and judges find when reviewing evidence. Defense attorney Bruce Nestor argued the government "lacks credible evidence to obtain an indictment before a grand jury and to avoid the embarrassment of making full discovery disclosures."

Rachel Moran, University of St. Thomas law professor, identified the core dynamic: "When somebody other than the agents has to look at evidence and decide whether an assault happened, in the vast majority of situations, they're deciding, no, there's no evidence of an assault."

## Retaliation Beyond Arrest

Arrest was not the only tool used against observers. Documented retaliation tactics included:

- **Facial Recognition Surveillance:** Agents used the [Mobile Fortify app](/entry/2026-02-06-ice-facial-recognition-surveillance-program) to scan observers' faces and build a database. Homan vowed to "make them famous."
- **Home Visits:** Agents followed [observers to their homes](/entry/2026-01-06-levy-observers-followed-home). In Maine, agents visited protesters' homes and told them: "This is a warning. We know where you live."
- **Global Entry Revocation:** [Nicole Cleland's](/entry/2026-01-10-nicole-cleland-global-entry-revoked) Global Entry and TSA PreCheck were revoked days after she was identified by facial recognition while observing.
- **Business Audits:** Mischief Toys, a St. Paul shop whose co-owner criticized ICE on ABC News, was targeted with an HSI investigation. Hennepin County Medical Center received a federal audit notice days after publicly criticizing warrantless entries.
- **Phone and Document Seizure:** A Minnesota Reformer [investigation](https://minnesotareformer.com/2026/02/12/why-is-ice-seizing-peoples-phones-and-documents/) documented agents systematically confiscating phones, driver's licenses, cash, and keys from observers and immigrants. Attorneys said no warrants had been issued for seized phones. Marine veteran Gust Johnson, 76, had his phone held at Whipple after being detained at the Alex Pretti scene; his daughter tracked the phone moving around the building and someone charged it days later. Another observer's iPhone 16 was seized January 21 and held as "evidence." Attorney Joshua Preston noted the government expressed interest in encrypted Signal group chats used to coordinate observer networks. Attorney Kevin Riach called the seizures potential Fourth Amendment violations. Immigration attorney Jennifer Scarborough said document seizures from immigrants were "another form of cruelty" — replacing a work permit costs over $500 and takes up to a year.
- **Rhetoric Escalation:** Federal officials progressively escalated their language about observers — from "agitators" to "insurrectionists" to, in at least one documented agent interaction, "domestic terrorists."

## Homan's Statements
In his press conference the same week, Homan described:
- Creation of a "multi-agency surge task force" and "unified joint operations center" to "leverage joint intelligence capabilities to effectively target threats"
- No reduction in militarized tactical teams guarding deportation operations "until we see a change in what's happening with the lawlessness"
- 158 people arrested for interfering with federal law enforcement in the past month; 85 cases accepted for prosecution (penalties range from 1 to 20 years)
- His desire to include people who follow ICE's activities in a government "database"
- An ultimatum to local leaders to "end the resistance"

## Context
Despite Homan's announced drawdown of 700 officers, approximately 2,000 federal personnel remain in the Twin Cities — 13 times the agencies' normal footprint. The night of Homan's drawdown address, 66 shackled passengers were loaded onto a deportation flight at MSP, the highest total in nearly two weeks.

Recher, who has monitored ICE since early January, noted that while agents have been less prone to immediately exit vehicles with guns drawn since the leadership change, the "one warning" policy represents a new and direct threat to observers' safety and constitutional rights.
